Where Does Word Information Live in Neural Space?

PCA dimensionality reduction & semantic deflation analysis of LOO neural retrieval

Methods

Vanilla retrieval: Leave-one-out nearest-centroid matching in raw neural feature space (high-gamma 70–150 Hz, lagged feature concatenation). Cosine distance.

PCA retrieval (Test 1): Per time bin, fit PCA on all trials' neural features and retain only the top-N principal components. Then run the same LOO nearest-centroid retrieval on the reduced feature set. No regression model is trained.

Deflated retrieval (Test 2): Per time bin, fit PLS regression from neural features to a semantic embedding (e.g., GloVe 300-D). The PLS x-rotations define the "semantic subspace". This subspace is orthogonally projected out of the neural features. LOO nearest-centroid retrieval is then run on the residual.

Significance: Paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on peak word balanced accuracy across patients.

Interpretation: Where Does Word Information Come From?

These two tests probe the structure of word-level neural representations and shed light on why vanilla LOO nearest-centroid retrieval in raw neural space achieves higher word accuracy than model-based semantic regression through word embeddings.

Test 1: PCA Dimensionality Reduction

PCA (10 PCs) retains 72.1% of vanilla peak word accuracy.

  • If PCA retains most accuracy (>80%), word-discriminable structure is concentrated in a low-dimensional subspace — the top principal components capture the axes along which word centroids separate.
  • If PCA loses substantial accuracy (<50%), word information is distributed across many dimensions and requires the full high-dimensional feature space.
  • PCA extracts the directions of maximum variance, not maximum class separability. If word boundaries align with high-variance directions (likely when different words activate distinct electrode populations), PCA preserves them. If word boundaries lie in low-variance directions, PCA will discard them.

Test 2: Semantic Subspace Deflation

Deflated (ConceptNet) retains 22.1% of vanilla peak word accuracy after removing its semantic subspace.

Deflated (FastText) retains 21.5% of vanilla peak word accuracy after removing its semantic subspace.

Deflated (GloVe) retains 23.4% of vanilla peak word accuracy after removing its semantic subspace.

Deflated (Word2Vec) retains 20.6% of vanilla peak word accuracy after removing its semantic subspace.

  • If deflation preserves word accuracy, the word-discriminating neural dimensions are largely orthogonal to the semantic embedding subspace. This means the LOO retrieval exploits neural features that encode word identity (e.g., phonological or articulatory codes) independently of the tested semantic space.
  • If deflation destroys word accuracy, word-discriminating structure substantially overlaps with the semantic subspace — the same neural dimensions that predict word embeddings also separate word centroids.
  • If different embeddings cause different deflation effects, it suggests those embedding spaces capture different aspects of word-level neural coding.

Why Does the Model Lose Word Accuracy?

Even when semantic subspaces overlap with word-separable structure, the regression model may lose word accuracy for several reasons:

  • Dimensionality bottleneck: The model projects neural features through a low-rank regression (e.g., PLS with 10 components or PCA to 10-D before Ridge), which discards neural dimensions that are word-discriminating but not well-predicted by the target embedding.
  • Embedding neighbourhood structure: Semantic embeddings group semantically-similar words close together (e.g., "cat" ≈ "dog"), collapsing word-level distinctions that the raw neural space preserves. The model's cosine retrieval in embedding space thus conflates within-category words.
  • Regression noise: The mapping from neural space to embedding space introduces prediction error that smears word centroids in embedding space, whereas LOO retrieval in neural space uses the raw centroids without a learned mapping.
  • Train/test mismatch: The regression model uses a train/test split, so some trials are never seen during training. The vanilla LOO centroid uses N−1 trials (all except the query), maximising the information per centroid.
